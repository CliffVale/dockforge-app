1 · What is molecular docking?

Proteins are tiny machines shaped like crumpled ribbons. Their shape is their function: hormones stick to shaped pockets on receptor proteins, enzymes grip molecules in active-site clefts, antibodies hug invaders. Docking is a computer simulation that asks: “If I had a small molecule (a ligand), where and how would it attach to this protein’s surface?”

The classic picture is lock and key: the protein pocket is the lock, the ligand is the key. Real biology adds a twist — both partners are floppy (“induced fit”), so docking must try many keys in slightly bending locks. The output is two things: a pose (the 3D position/orientation of the ligand) and a score (a number estimating how tightly it binds). Drug hunters use it to triage millions of candidate molecules before ever mixing one in a test tube.

In the lock-and-key picture, the “key” is…

Correct: the ligand is the key; the pocket is the lock. (And real keys and locks both flex a little!)

A docking run gives you two things. They are…

Correct: the pose (geometry of binding) and the score (estimated strength).

2 · The players: proteins and ligands

To dock, you need a 3D structure of both players, usually as a PDB file — a plain-text list of every atom with x/y/z coordinates. Protein structures come mainly from X-ray crystallography, cryo-EM or NMR experiments, and are shared in the RCSB Protein Data Bank (230,000+ entries, each with a 4-character code like 1BNA). If no structure exists, computers can predict one — that’s what AlphaFold made famous.

Ligands are small molecules: a drug candidate, a nutrient, a fragment. You can describe them compactly as a SMILES string — e.g. caffeine is Cn1cnc2c1c(=O)n(C)c(=O)n2C — which software converts to 3D. In a PDB file, protein atoms live in ATOM records and ligand atoms in HETATM records. Before docking, structures are “prepared”: incomplete side chains fixed, hydrogens added, waters usually removed (except the odd one that matters!). And targets aren’t only proteins — DNA is a docking target too: many antibiotics and cancer drugs work by slotting into DNA’s minor groove, which is exactly the example you’ll dock in the Lab.

A PDB file is…

Correct: plain text, one line per atom, with coordinates — you can open it in any text editor.

Which string compactly encodes a small molecule’s atoms and bonds?

Correct: SMILES. (FASTA encodes protein/DNA *sequences* — no 3D, no bond info.)

3 · Binding pockets: where molecules stick

Proteins are lumpy. Some lumps are binding pockets — cavities whose size, shape and chemistry make small molecules happy to sit there. Hydrophobic pockets hug greasy groups; charged pockets welcome oppositely charged parts; deep narrow clefts grip long skinny molecules.

How do you find the pocket? Three ways: (1) co-crystal evidence — if the crystallographers captured a ligand inside, you already know where it binds (this is what our DNA example uses); (2) geometry software — tools like P2Rank scan the surface for enclosed cavities and rank their “druggability”; (3) knowledge — a conserved active site (e.g. a kinase hinge) tells you where to look. Docking into the wrong pocket is one of the most common beginner mistakes — the best score in a meaningless pocket is still meaningless.

The fastest way to know a protein’s real binding site?

Correct: a co-crystallized ligand is direct experimental evidence of the site. Best-score hunting can mislead.

Which pocket property does NOT make a ligand more likely to bind there?

Correct: what matters is complementarity — shape + chemistry. “Deepest pocket” isn’t a binding rule.

4 · Poses and scores: what the computer actually does

Docking is a search plus a judgment. The search (often a clever random walk called Monte Carlo, plus genetic algorithms) generates thousands of candidate poses — ligand here flipped, ligand there bent. The judgment is the scoring function: a fast physics-flavored formula estimating each pose’s favorability from hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, shape complementarity, electrostatics, and penalties for strain or clashes.

Vina’s scores come out in roughly kcal/mol, more negative = tighter predicted binding. A rough rhyme for drug-like molecules: −6 is decent, −8 is interesting, −11 would make you double-check your preparation. Crucial honesty: scores are relative and error-prone — great for ranking similar compounds in the same pocket, unreliable as absolute binding energies, and no substitute for experiments.

Vina reports −6.2 for ligand A and −8.9 for ligand B (same pocket, same protocol). What’s the reasonable read?

Correct: more negative ≈ more favorable prediction. Scores rank; they don’t hand you exact energies.

A scoring function estimates…

Correct: it weighs H-bonds, hydrophobic contact, electrostatics, clashes and strain into one number.

5 · Preparing molecules: PDBQT and friends

Docking engines are picky eaters. Vina wants PDBQT files — like PDB but with partial charges (Q) and atom types (T) added. Preparing a ligand means: generate a sensible 3D shape, add hydrogens, pick protonation states (is that amine +1 or neutral at pH 7.4?), choose tautomers, and mark which bonds can rotate. Preparing a receptor means: repair missing atoms, add hydrogens, assign charges.

Why the fuss? A missing hydrogen or the wrong protonation state changes every predicted H-bond. Two hydrogens at a clash instead of one — instant garbage score. Classic prep tools: mk_prepare_ligand.py (Meeko) for ligands, PDB2PQR/PDBFixer for receptors. Our Webina lab includes a converter, so you can often start from plain PDB/SDF/MOL2 — but understanding what got fixed makes you a better scientist, not just a button-pusher.

What does the “QT” in PDBQT add to a normal PDB file?

Correct: partial Charges and atom Types — the extras Vina’s scoring needs.

Skipping ligand protonation (picking the right ionization state) most directly breaks…

Correct: wrong ionization → wrong charge pattern → wrong H-bonds → misleading scores.

6 · The grid box: telling the search where to look

Docking doesn’t search the whole universe — you define a grid box: the 3D region of space around the protein where ligand poses may appear. Box placement is strategy: too small and you’ve excluded the real answer; too large and you dilute the search (and slow it down) while inviting false pockets.

The standard practice: center the box on the known pocket and pad ~8–12 Å beyond the co-crystallized ligand in every direction. If you have no site knowledge, you can run blind docking — a box enclosing the entire protein surface — which Webina can do but which costs time and reliability at larger proteins. The other key knob is exhaustiveness (Vina’s sampling effort): 8 is quick learning territory; 32+ is for results you’d write down.

A good box for docking into a known site…

Correct: pocket-centered, comfortably padded — focused but roomy.

Raising exhaustiveness mainly…

Correct: it’s the search-effort dial — better sampling, more time. Scores’ meaning doesn’t change.

7 · Reading results like a scientist

Vina outputs a table of modes (poses): each with a score and three RMSD values — distances (in Å) from the best-scoring pose. Pose 1 is the winner; lower modes are alternatives. The three RMSD columns tell you how much the search agrees with itself: if the top several poses cluster tightly, the result is more trustworthy than a scattered mess of similar scores.

Then the fun part: interrogate the pose. Which residues hug the ligand? Are there hydrogen bonds? Salt bridges? π-stacking? In the Result Viewer you’ll load your poses over the pocket and see. For published-quality claims, scientists add redocking validation (can the engine recover the crystal ligand’s position? aim for RMSD < 2 Å) and pose-validity checks (PoseBusters). Always end by asking: does this make chemical sense, and what experiment would test it?

RMSD values in the results table measure…

Correct: RMSD = average atom-to-atom distance between poses — a spread/consistency measure.

“Redocking the crystal ligand and hoping for RMSD < 2 Å” is…

Correct: if the engine can’t re-find a known answer, don’t trust it on unknown ones.
